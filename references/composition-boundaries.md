# Uncanny Relational Composition Grammar

Do not choose a fixed named template. Compose one coherent visual whole whose governing purpose is to break order, create abnormal spatial logic, and sustain uncanny tension.

## 1. State one visual proposition

Before placing material, describe the new image silently in one sentence. The sentence should name the protagonist and the impossible spatial condition, for example:

- a bicycle blocks the entrance to a corridor that keeps returning into itself;
- several stair sections grow into one unsupported building;
- palace roofs expand into an architecture that cannot exist;
- a tree interrupts and carries pieces of a room through open space.

If the result can only be described as “several extracted objects on a background,” the reconstruction is not developed enough. If it can be described as a plausible enlarged building or normal scene, the abnormal proposition is also too weak.

## 2. Establish hierarchy

Use one protagonist. Make it dominant through one or more of:

- scale;
- silhouette;
- position;
- contrast;
- directional control;
- uniqueness;
- repetition used as emphasis;
- control of the main spatial path.

Use only one or two dominant protagonist anchors. Smaller echoes of the same motif count as secondary material and may reinforce scale rhythm. Large secondary material is allowed, but each secondary family may appear no more than four times. The test is not size alone: the protagonist must remain the first and strongest remembered form.

Default to a combined visual center near the middle region of the canvas. The main anchor may be offset, cropped by an edge, or asymmetrical, but secondary counterweights must keep the total composition from collapsing into a corner.

## 3. Choose an overall structure

Select only what suits the source:

### Mass distribution

- concentrated core;
- asymmetrical counterweight;
- top sparse and bottom dense;
- top dense and bottom sparse;
- unequal split field;
- edge invasion;
- connected islands;
- full-field pressure with one hierarchy.

### Movement path

- diagonal crossing;
- vertical cascade;
- horizontal progression;
- arc or orbit;
- radial growth;
- S-curve;
- perspective tunnel;
- zigzag return;
- looping path;
- frame or plane progression.

These are compositional resources, not modes. Do not force every image into one of them when another source-led structure is clearer.

## 4. Break the source order

Identify the source's dominant ordering system before composing. Highly regular, symmetrical, repetitive, frontal, gridded, or architecturally continuous sources require deliberate counter-order.

Break at least one governing rule through a source-responsive relationship such as:

- interrupted symmetry;
- sudden scale discontinuity;
- a tilted bounded plane cutting through a frontal structure;
- a perspective path that reconnects at an impossible level;
- a repeated sequence that changes direction or loses one expected interval;
- a clean seam separating two apparently connected structures;
- an unsupported or impossible connection;
- a cropped element resuming from a nonmatching position.

Do not treat random rotation, inversion, scatter, or distortion as automatic solutions. The break must strengthen the proposition and remain visually controlled.

## 5. Build a relationship network

The composition is not a diagram in which every secondary element points toward the protagonist. Build a network:

- the protagonist controls the main impression;
- some secondary elements connect directly to it;
- others connect to one another as a subplot, counterweight, interruption, or spatial clue;
- negative space and boundaries may also participate in relationships.

Each important element must have at least one relationship, but not necessarily with the protagonist.

Useful relationships include overlap, concealment, contact, continuation, opposition, enclosure, scale cue, emergence, repetition, and directional echo.

## 6. Use local independence without isolation

Secondary groups may remain independently readable and momentarily attract attention. They can disagree with the protagonist's direction, interrupt its shape, or create a separate micro-scene.

Independence becomes failure when pieces are evenly spaced, framed as specimens, or unrelated except for sharing the same source photograph.

Vary scale and completeness across the ensemble. A large anchor, smaller architectural echoes, one substantial organic mass, and one readable spatial plane can create richness without equal competition.

## 7. Integrate a spatial engine when useful

A corridor, stair, doorway, road, bridge, wall, railing, or perspective plane may carry the viewing path. It can be stretched, bent, repeated, looped, contained, or redirected.

Keep the spatial engine subordinate to the protagonist unless it is itself the chosen protagonist. Use contrast, detail, crop, overlap, and placement to maintain that distinction.

## 8. Create a whole, not a repaired reality

The final work may resemble one impossible building, room, object, landscape, or machine. This is valid and often preferable to isolated fragments.

Overall coherence may come from:

- a shared silhouette;
- continuous visual growth;
- interlocking source forms;
- one dominant path;
- recurring curves, edges, or colors;
- layered occlusion;
- one connected cluster with a smaller independent counter-group.

Physical continuity may be impossible. Visual continuity must remain convincing.

Do not restore the source's original scene, perspective order, or full background. Do not break a strong reconstructed whole into unrelated fragments, but do preserve one or two localized visible clean seams or cuts so the whole never becomes a seamless realistic render.

## 9. Control density, balance, negative space, and boundaries

- Replace the intact source background with a clean non-scenic field or a newly constructed spatial system.
- Preserve breathing space around and within the active composition, but do not create a large dead zone by pushing every element into one corner.
- Keep the combined visual weight near the canvas center by default. If the protagonist is strongly offset, use smaller echoes or independent secondary masses to restore balance.
- Do not allow the protagonist family, repeated architecture, vegetation, or any other material group to grow into a large uninterrupted mass that fills most of the canvas.
- Do not answer a dense source by using most of its available material. Select aggressively and stop when the proposition is complete.
- Keep negative space concentrated rather than distributing equal gaps around every piece.
- A scenic fragment may be bounded, stretched, cropped, entered, obscured, or connected into another structure; it must not reproduce the intact source scene.
- Use one or two clearly visible clean hard cuts, narrow white seams, geometric crops, tilted image edges, natural silhouettes meeting flat cuts, or purposeful abrupt boundaries. Keep these local rather than outlining every piece.
- Match boundaries to material. Use geometric crops and white seams mainly for architecture or spatial planes. Let trees and foliage form organic silhouettes through masking, overlap, and composite growth.
- Do not add torn-paper edges, paper holes, kraft textures, or artificial damage.

## 10. Preserve collage identity without fragmentation theater

Collage must be evident through two complementary forms: one or two localized visible clean boundaries and one changed relationship such as scale, overlap, perspective, continuity, containment, recursion, or impossible joining. Do not demand additional signals after these already make the reconstruction legible.

Avoid:

- isolated sticker layouts;
- equal-sized panels;
- several unrelated focal points;
- scrapbook arrangements;
- decorative grids of secondary objects;
- global paper or vintage treatment;
- seamless restoration of the original scene;
- random visual tricks that do not strengthen the proposition.
