# Reconstruction Operations

Operations are optional tools for building one uncanny whole. None is a mandatory effect, but the combined result must visibly break the source's order and read as abnormal rather than merely decorative or rearranged.

## Core assembly operations

- **Isolation:** separate a source element from its original surroundings.
- **Crop:** keep the part that contributes to the new relationship.
- **Scale shift:** change visual weight or perceived distance.
- **Overlap:** let one element cover or interrupt another.
- **Containment:** place a transformed scene or element inside a deliberate boundary.
- **Partial concealment:** build depth while keeping recognition.
- **Edge entry:** let a large element enter or leave the canvas.
- **Connection:** align source-compatible edges into a new physical or visual relation.
- **Reorientation:** rotate or redirect a non-human form when direction benefits.
- **Stretch:** extend a stable form to create impossible distance, motion, or proportion.
- **Repetition:** reuse a form or family to create emphasis, rhythm, depth, looping, or growth.
- **Recursion:** let a form or spatial passage reappear inside itself or along its own depth.
- **Suspension:** remove expected support while maintaining compositional balance.
- **Inversion:** turn a selected non-human form upside down only when it strengthens the whole.
- **Directed dispersal:** separate related fragments along a readable path or density change.
- **Impossible connection:** make stairs, walls, corridors, buildings, vehicles, or objects join in a physically impossible but visually convincing way.

Every retained element must be structurally recontextualized. A filter, paper texture, border, or mere removal of the background does not count.

## Operation decision test

Before applying a strong operation, ask:

1. Does it strengthen or clarify the protagonist?
2. Does it improve rhythm, tension, depth, or the overall proposition?
3. Does the source form remain recognizable enough?
4. Does it create a relationship rather than an isolated trick?
5. Is the composition better with it than without it?

Omit an operation when it fails this test. Repetition, inversion, stretch, rotation, dispersal, recursion, and scale conflict have equal status; none should become a default response to a subject category.

## Build abnormality through relationships

The result needs one unmistakable abnormal spatial proposition, not a checklist of effects. A viewer should perceive the broken logic before needing an explanation. It may emerge from:

- a corridor that extends behind an object beyond plausible distance;
- stairs connecting incompatible levels;
- architecture growing through itself;
- repeated roofs changing scale as one structure;
- an object blocking, entering, or producing a secondary space;
- a railing continuing into a wall or unrelated depth;
- a local perspective that contradicts another while remaining compositionally connected.

Prefer a small number of mutually reinforcing changes. Remove decorative anomalies that compete with hierarchy or rhythm.

For a highly regular source, begin by naming its strongest order—symmetry, repetition, alignment, continuous perspective, stable scale, or structural support—then choose an operation that breaks that order. Merely enlarging or neatly cascading the same forms is insufficient when it still resembles plausible architecture or normal perspective.

When splitting one structure, a visible seam alone is not a strong anomaly. Make the split alter at least two meaningful relationships: offset, scale, angle, depth, containment, continuation, or reconnection. Keep the intervention localized so the rest of the composition can remain harmonious.

## Preserve recognition

After strong transformation, retain the cues needed to identify important source material: silhouette, color, surface texture, edge rhythm, familiar components, or spatial function.

Do not distort faces, bodies, hands, or identity-bearing anatomy by default. People may be relocated, conservatively repeated, cropped, partially concealed, or used to establish scale.

## Relationships before fragments

Favor:

- joining;
- passing behind or through;
- interruption;
- enclosure;
- emergence;
- recession;
- shared directional rhythm;
- controlled opposition.

Avoid producing separate pieces merely to demonstrate cutting. However, do not blend all pieces into one seamless rendered scene. Preserve one or two local clean crop boundaries, narrow white seams, tilted image edges, or deliberate cuts where reconstructed systems meet. Do not apply the same boundary treatment everywhere.

Use material-responsive boundaries:

- architectural and scenic planes may use crisp geometric crops or white seams;
- trees and foliage should usually keep organic silhouettes, interlock through masking, or grow across adjacent material;
- linear details may cross boundaries, terminate abruptly, or bridge mismatched depths;
- people and crowds should normally remain a readable local scene rather than being sliced into arbitrary panels.

## Repetition behavior

Repetition has no required minimum. The protagonist uses one or two dominant anchors; smaller motif echoes are secondary material, and each secondary family may appear no more than four times. If repetition is used, vary only what helps the proposition: scale, crop, spacing, overlap, completeness, orientation, or depth.

One large and one small instance can reinforce recognition, but this is an option, not a rule. Repetition may also be assigned to a secondary element while the protagonist remains unique.

Stop repeating as soon as the next instance adds density without adding a new relationship, rhythm, depth change, interruption, or abnormality. Never extend one repeat family into a large continuous mass covering most of the image.

## Spatial-engine behavior

When a corridor, stair, bridge, road, wall, railing, or doorway organizes the image, transform its depth or direction without letting it monopolize the entire composition. Integrate it with the protagonist through overlap, framing, interruption, emergence, or a shared vanishing movement.

If it becomes more memorable than the intended protagonist, reduce its contrast, crop it, conceal it, redirect it, or shorten its visible extent.

## Visible reconstruction evidence

Every accepted result must contain both:

1. one or two localized clean visible collage boundaries: a narrow white seam, crisp geometric crop, tilted image edge, natural silhouette meeting a flat cut, or purposeful abrupt boundary;
2. a changed relationship: scale jump, perspective conflict, impossible join, stretch, recursion, discontinuity, containment, or boundary crossing.

These are structural signals, not decoration. Do not multiply them across every element, do not force organic material into rectangles, and do not convert them into torn-paper damage.

## Excluded visual treatment

Do not use torn edges, paper holes, kraft paper, yellowed paper, scrapbook texture, newspaper grain, global fading, warm vintage grading, full-image noise, pixelation, color inversion, or external decoration in this version.
