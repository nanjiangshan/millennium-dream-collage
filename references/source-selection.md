# Source Selection and Visual Roles

Select material by what it can contribute to one new visual proposition, not merely by size or object count.

## 1. Choose one protagonist

Every result has one visual protagonist. It may be an object, person, animal, building, stair, roof family, vehicle, tree, or another source-specific form. Represent it with one or two dominant anchor instances only. A two-anchor protagonist remains one visual system, not two equal focal points. Smaller motif echoes are secondary material rather than additional protagonists.

The protagonist must:

- be identifiable at first glance;
- carry more visual weight than any single secondary element;
- determine the main silhouette, direction, rhythm, or spatial claim;
- remain recognizable after reconstruction.

Do not assign a fixed percentage. Dominance may come from scale, placement, contrast, isolation, repetition, directional control, or uniqueness. Use size variation when useful: one dominant anchor may coexist with smaller echoes without losing hierarchy. Do not enlarge or repeat it until it becomes one continuous field covering most of the composition.

## 2. Inventory secondary material

Inspect the source for:

- people, crowds, animals, and gestures;
- buildings, vehicles, furniture, products, and vessels;
- trees, plants, water, clouds, and terrain;
- railings, walls, stairs, doors, roof edges, roads, cables, and handles;
- tiles, masonry, windows, reflections, signs, and surface details;
- corridors, courtyards, rooms, streets, doorways, bridges, and other spatial fragments.

Never treat the complete photograph as a candidate.

Different materials require different extraction logic:

- architecture, walls, courtyards, rooms, and other spatial planes may tolerate geometric crops, clean seams, or tilted bounded fragments;
- trees, foliage, clouds, crowds, animals, and other organic forms should normally retain natural silhouettes or combine through masking and overlap;
- railings, roads, branches, roof ridges, and handles work as linear connectors, interruptions, or directional counterweights;
- small buildings, roofs, windows, products, or other motif echoes may reinforce the protagonist through varied scale without becoming equal focal points.

## 3. Give secondary material independent roles

Secondary elements may:

- support or frame the protagonist;
- oppose its direction, color, or mass;
- interrupt or conceal part of it;
- connect two otherwise separate groups;
- establish human scale;
- open a secondary space;
- form a small independent subplot;
- create rhythm, texture, or a pause in the viewing path.

They do not all need to point toward, touch, or directly serve the protagonist. However, each important secondary element must relate to at least one other element, group, boundary, void, or spatial path. Remove isolated display pieces that add no relationship.

Each secondary material family may appear no more than four times. This is a maximum, not a target. Stop earlier when another instance adds density without adding a new relationship, rhythm, tension, or piece of information.

## 4. Find a spatial engine

A corridor, stair, road, bridge, doorway, wall, railing, repeated façade, or perspective ground may organize depth and motion. Treat it as a spatial engine when stretching, bending, repetition, containment, or redirection can produce an impossible spatial experience.

The spatial engine can be visually large, but it must not erase the protagonist's identity or become the only thing the viewer remembers.

## 5. Decide whether repetition helps

Similar visible forms are only a candidate repeat family. Use repetition when it strengthens recognition, rhythm, accumulation, recession, looping, crowding, or abnormal growth.

Do not repeat because:

- the source happens to contain many similar objects;
- a quota must be filled;
- empty space needs decoration;
- the protagonist is expected to appear twice.

Repetition may involve the protagonist or a secondary family, or it may be omitted entirely.

## 6. Select for relationships, not quotas

Use enough source-derived material to create hierarchy, rhythm, and secondary discovery, but stop before density overwhelms the composition. Richness comes from varied sizes and relationships rather than a high number of equal fragments. The one-to-two dominant-anchor limit and four-per-secondary-family limit are ceilings; do not fill them automatically.

Before retaining an element, identify at least one intended relationship: contact, overlap, concealment, continuation, enclosure, opposition, scale cue, repetition, directional echo, or spatial conflict.

## 7. Audit the source order

Identify what makes the source visually orderly: symmetry, repeated spacing, one-point perspective, continuous architecture, aligned horizons, stable scale, or a regular grid. For a highly orderly source, select material that can break at least one of these governing rules without destroying recognition.

Do not answer a dense source with more density or a repeated source with automatic repetition. The transformation should counter the source's dominant order when that order would otherwise make the result feel normal.

## Protected meaning

Cropping and relocation may change spatial relationships, but must not invent a false identity, brand, event, or personal history. Preserve recognizable people and avoid accidental anatomical merging or distortion.
