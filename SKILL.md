# Millennium Dream Collage / 千禧梦核拼贴

Treat the supplied photo as a donor sheet, never as the finished canvas. Rebuild selected source material into one new visual proposition organized around uncanniness, abnormality, and the deliberate breaking of the source's visual order. The result must read as spatially wrong at first glance while remaining compositionally coherent.

The foundation is **reconstruction**, not fragmentation. The result may show clean crops and impossible joins, but must not become torn-paper craft, a collection of isolated stickers, or a seamless reconstruction of the original scene.

This first version focuses on source selection, hierarchy, relational assembly, spatial tension, and composition. Do not add pixelation, color inversion, decorative external assets, legacy UI, generated text, global aging, or paper effects unless the user explicitly asks.

## Read the rules

- Read `references/source-selection.md` before selecting the protagonist and secondary material.
- Read `references/transformation-grammar.md` before choosing reconstruction operations.
- Read `references/composition-boundaries.md` before designing the layout.
- Read `references/acceptance-checks.md` before returning an image.
- Read `references/test-plan.md` only when evaluating or revising the skill.

## Build a private Composition Plan

Before generating, silently decide:

1. the single visual protagonist, built from only one or two dominant anchors, with any smaller motif echoes treated as secondary material;
2. what makes it dominant: scale, placement, contrast, silhouette, repetition, or directional control;
3. which secondary elements deserve independent presence;
4. how each important element relates to at least one other element, group, boundary, void, or spatial path;
5. whether the source contains a useful spatial engine such as a corridor, stair, road, bridge, doorway, wall, railing, or perspective ground;
6. one overall abnormal proposition that breaks a specific source order and can be perceived at first glance;
7. one primary mass distribution and one movement path, plus only the depth and containment relationships that strengthen that proposition;
8. enough breathing space around a composition whose combined visual center remains near the canvas center, unless an intentional counterweight clearly stabilizes an offset;
9. which optional abnormal operations genuinely improve hierarchy, rhythm, tension, or spatial meaning;
10. which one or two local clean crop boundaries or white seams will make the reconstruction visibly collaged without outlining every element;
11. whether repetition is useful; omit it when it is merely decorative or expected;
12. what must be removed so the original scene and its original spatial order cannot survive.

Do not expose the Composition Plan unless the user requests it.

## Select one protagonist and an ensemble

- Never select the complete photograph as an element.
- Every image has one visual protagonist, represented by one or two dominant anchor instances only. Two anchors may form one pair or system; they must not become two competing focal points. Smaller echoes of the same motif count as secondary material and remain subject to the four-instance secondary ceiling. Do not impose a fixed size percentage.
- The protagonist must be the first remembered element and determine the main silhouette, direction, rhythm, or spatial claim.
- For a simple photo, keep only the material that helps build a new proposition. For a rich photo, select several different semantic families or visual roles without turning the rule into a quota. Each secondary material family may appear no more than four times, and four is a ceiling rather than a target.
- Secondary material may support, oppose, interrupt, connect, frame, conceal, establish scale, open another space, or form a small independent subplot.
- Secondary elements do not all need to point toward or directly touch the protagonist.
- Each important secondary element must participate in a visual relationship with the protagonist, another secondary group, a boundary, a void, or the spatial engine. Remove orphaned display pieces.
- A large secondary element is allowed when hierarchy remains legible.
- Use varied scale to build hierarchy: a dominant anchor may be accompanied by several smaller secondary echoes when they strengthen rhythm and balance.
- Similar forms are a potential repeat family, not an automatic instruction to repeat them.

Follow `references/source-selection.md`.

## Reconstruct relationships

- Isolate, crop, enlarge, reduce, overlap, contain, conceal, redirect, connect, repeat, stretch, rotate, suspend, or distort source-derived material only when it changes the visual relationship.
- Every retained element must be relocated or structurally recontextualized. A filter or border does not count.
- Favor interactions: joining, passing behind, interrupting, enclosing, emerging from, receding into, or sharing a directional rhythm.
- Use the source's spatial engine to organize depth or motion when helpful, but do not let it replace the protagonist.
- Repetition, inversion, stretch, rotation, dispersal, recursion, scale conflict, and impossible connection are optional operations of equal status. None is mandatory.
- Before using a strong operation, ask whether it strengthens the protagonist, improves rhythm or tension, preserves recognition, and helps the whole. Omit it if the composition is stronger without it.
- The image must contain one unmistakable abnormal relation that breaks the source's scale, continuity, direction, perspective, symmetry, repetition, support, or connection. It need not correspond to a named effect or a fixed number of operations, but it must not be explainable as normal perspective or ordinary arrangement.
- When the source is highly regular, symmetrical, repetitive, or architectural, deliberately break at least one governing order rather than extending it more neatly.
- If an element is split, make the intervention structurally decisive. Change at least two meaningful relationships among offset, scale, angle, depth, containment, continuation, or reconnection; a narrow gap between otherwise aligned parts is not enough.
- Preserve at least the recognition cues needed to identify the protagonist and important source material.
- Do not distort identity-bearing human anatomy by default.

Follow `references/transformation-grammar.md`.

## Compose one whole with local independence

- Never choose a fixed named layout mode.
- Establish one readable overall silhouette, movement, cluster, or spatial progression. Keep the combined visual center near the middle region by default; an offset protagonist requires secondary counterweights that restore overall balance.
- Let local groups retain character and partial independence. Hierarchy is not total obedience: a tree may oppose a building, a crowd may form its own scene, and a railing may interrupt rather than point.
- Make enough elements touch, overlap, conceal, connect, interrupt, enclose, or share a path that the result reads as reconstruction rather than inventory.
- Preserve useful breathing space around the active composition. Do not create a large dead zone by pushing the entire cluster into a corner, do not insert equal gaps around every fragment, and do not fill the field simply because more source material is available.
- Physical logic may be wrong; visual logic must be right.
- The result may resemble one impossible building, room, object, or landscape when that is the successful proposition. Do not break a strong whole merely to prove that collage occurred.
- Preserve collage identity through both: (a) one or two localized clean crop boundaries, narrow white seams, tilted image edges, or deliberate cuts; and (b) at least one changed relationship such as scale jump, perspective conflict, impossible join, interruption, stretch, recursion, or discontinuity. Do not outline or panelize every material. Do not use torn edges, paper damage, or random separation.
- Match the cut language to the material: scenic and architectural planes may use clean geometric crops or white seams; trees, foliage, people, and other organic material should normally use natural silhouettes, overlap, masking, or composite growth rather than forced rectangular slicing.
- Replace the intact source background with a clean non-scenic field or a newly constructed spatial system. Any retained scenic fragment must be transformed and must not restore the source layout.

Follow `references/composition-boundaries.md`.

## Visual treatment

- Preserve the useful photographic color, clarity, light, and surface identity of source material.
- Default to a clean, quiet, untextured base field whose color supports the source. A light neutral field is acceptable but not mandatory.
- Use clean hard crops, geometric cuts, natural silhouettes, or purposeful abrupt boundaries as needed.
- Do not add torn-paper edges, paper holes, kraft paper, yellowed paper, scrapbook texture, watercolor paper, newspaper grain, global fading, warm vintage grading, or full-image noise.
- Do not make the image uniformly cozy, nostalgic, decorative, or handmade. The emotional base is uncanny, spatially wrong, and visually controlled.

## Generate and verify

1. Use the supplied photograph as the edit reference and donor sheet.
2. Form the prompt in this order: source rejection contract; protagonist; secondary ensemble; spatial engine; overall proposition; selected operations; hierarchy and relationships; visual treatment; exclusions.
3. Generate one newly reconstructed collage with the available image editing tool.
4. Inspect it against `references/acceptance-checks.md`.
5. If a hard gate fails, make one focused retry naming only the failed property and repeat the source rejection contract.
6. Return the image with a short Chinese note describing the protagonist, the independent secondary relationships, and the overall spatial proposition.

## Non-negotiable rules

- The intact source photograph cannot be the final canvas, full background, or preserved composition.
- The output has exactly one visual protagonist, represented by one or two dominant anchors only. Smaller echoes may appear as secondary material when they support hierarchy and rhythm.
- Each secondary material family may appear no more than four times. All counts are ceilings, never quotas.
- Secondary material retains local independence but cannot degrade into unrelated display pieces.
- The final image must form one describable reconstructed whole with an unmistakable abnormal relation that visibly breaks the source's order.
- Highly regular sources require a deliberate break in symmetry, continuity, perspective, rhythm, scale, or connection.
- The composition must not turn one subject or repeat family into a large uninterrupted field. Keep density restrained, preserve useful breathing space, and keep the combined visual center near the middle unless a clear counterweight stabilizes an offset.
- The reconstruction must show one or two localized clean collage boundaries and at least one altered spatial or structural relationship. Do not apply the same boundary treatment to every material.
- Organic secondary material must not be forced into rectangular panels merely to prove collage; let it build shape through silhouette, masking, overlap, or interconnection.
- A chosen split must be bold enough to alter at least two structural relationships. A thin white gap between otherwise aligned pieces is not a sufficient anomaly.
- Repetition and every named anomaly operation are optional and source-responsive.
- Do not damage a successful hierarchy or rhythm merely to add inversion, stretching, scattering, fragmentation, or another visible trick.
- Do not rely on torn edges, exposed paper gaps, kraft texture, global filters, or uniform aging to communicate collage or millennium mood.
- Do not add pixelation, color inversion, external clip art, old phones, birds, leaves, suns, decorative text, or complex UI by default in this version.
- Do not produce a filter-only edit, a scrapbook, an evenly scattered sticker sheet, a material catalogue, or several unrelated focal points.
